A B S T R A C T  
Solar photovoltaic technologies are increasingly implemented in airport premises. In certain conditions of sun 
path, the glare from solar photovoltaic modules may the reduce visibility of pilots and air traffic controllers. 
Despite the threat to aviation safety with solar installations in airport, only a few countries have framed regu­
lation on glare impact. The paper attempts to study the various factors affecting the occurrence of glare from 
solar PV array in Airport. The main objective is to review the approaches and metrics for assessing the glare 
impact from the solar photovoltaic array. In addition, the work summarises the glare guidelines existing in USA, 
Canada and European nations. The main factors that influence the glare occurrence are suns position, tilt angle, 
Surface texture, color and location of PV module. It was concluded that the existing glare assessment approaches 
are of limited practical use in the worldwide scenario. Though there are several limitations with the existing 
glare indices, the Ho et al metrics is reasonably a good indicator for glare assessment in airport solar PV in­
stallations. Guidelines formulated by Federal Aviation Administration, (FAA), USA is good enough to be used as 
guideline for glare impact assessment in other countries. It is envisaged that this work will assist the research 
communities for the development of the globally acceptable glare assessment and metrics.   
1. Introduction 
Solar PV based energy generation is in the limelight in various lo­
cations across the world (J¨ager-Waldau, 2020). Solar PV systems are 
suitable in airport premises, mainly due to the vast & shade-free spaces 
and huge energy requirement. Also, an on-site solar PV system helps to 
reduce the energy bill and to mitigate carbon emissions of an airport 
(Sreenath et al., 2019). Besides, these clean energy initiatives in the 
airport are in line with environmental policies of the concerned legis­
lative bodies. A handful of airports around the globe have installed solar 
PV systems in their premises which is low when compared to the total 
number of airports. Solar PV systems are commissioned in airports such 
as Cochin airport (India), Chicago Rockford Airport (USA), Fresno 
Yosemite Airport (USA), Indianapolis Airport (USA), Adelaide airport 
(Australia), Kuala Lumpur Airport (Malaysia), Mumbai airport (India), 
Moi airport (Kenya) etc. (Sreenath et al., 2020; Sreenath et al., 2021). 
Solar reflections are seen in everyday life. It can be from glass fa­
cades, solar PV modules, and even art installations (Danks et al., 2016). 
The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) reported that glare from 
direct sunlight contributed to nearly a dozen aviation accidents on 
average each year (Zhu, 2018). The front surface of Solar PV modules is 
made from glass material. Sunlight reflected from the glass covering is 
likely to cause glare (Protogeropoulos and Zachariou, 2010). The issue 
of reflections from PV installations has been discussed in certain legis­
lative documents concerning environmental matters. Practically, this 
could become a major barrier for the development of solar PV systems, 
especially in sensitive areas such as civil and military airports, urban 
areas, etc. The potential for glare from the solar PV system is a concern 
to the safe operation of an airport. The airport safety is at risk if the 
visual performance of the pilot or air traffic controllers is reduced. The 
solar PV glare may cause dazzling to pilots. Sometimes, it may confuse 
the pilots with aeronautical lights. The issues of solar PV glare in airport 
area is reported in news and websites (Federal Aviation Administration 
(FAA), 2018). The glare from the solar canopy project in Manchester- 
Boston Regional airport affected the visibility of officials in the air 
traffic control tower. It is reported that solar PV panels of USD 3.5

The different approaches and metrics to assess glare differs with 
different scenarios such as indoor lighting, daylighting or direct sun­
light. Many literatures studied the glare aspects from indoor lighting and 
daylighting environment (particularly glare from facades). In a review 
carried out by (Danks et al., 2016), the existing regulations and metrics

discussed. However, this review is focussed on glare impact from

literatures published in the area of solar PV glare are read and processed.

hence there exists a lack of clarity. In addition, the application of solar

regulation) of solar PV glare especially in the airport environment. Solar 
PV systems are expected to be deployed in many airports across the

affect the growth of solar PV systems in the airport environment. The 
main objectives are  
• To review various factors influencing reflections from the solar PV

• To summarise the approaches and metrics related to glare assessment 
in airport  
• To compare solar glare guidelines existing in different countries

2. Theory on light reflection 
When a ray of light transits from one medium to another with a 
different refractive index (RI), an optical phenomenon called reflection 
takes place (Raut et al., 2011). The fraction of incident light reflected at 
the interface is measured by reflectance, R. According to Augustin-Jean 
Fresnel, the remaining part is transmitted, which is measured by 
transmittance, T. 
2.1. Solar PV reflections 
Solar PV modules are made to absorb maximum sunlight when sun 
rays strike glass material on the PV module; it converses an air-glass 
interface (Shukla et al., 2016). Most of the sunlight enters into the 
glass medium since the material is transparent. Then the sun rays pass 
through the ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) layer, suffering negligible 
reflection at the interface. Finally, the solar rays reach the PV cell, and 
the photoelectric effect occurs (Fig. 1). Generally, the reflection from the 
solar PV module is said to remain less than 5% (Khan et al., 2019). Even 
1% illumination of direct sunlight on a surface is similar to the bright­
ness from a 60 W lamp. (Labib & Baltazar, 2014) reported about the 
scarcity of reflection data for solar PV modules from manufacturers. 
Protogeropoulos and Zachariou estimated the reflectivity of the PV 
module located at Athens, Greece, with the help of an accurate spec­
trometer. They reported a PV module reflectance of 15% for incident 
angles up to 45o (Protogeropoulos and Zachariou, 2010). The reflection 
of sunlight can be correlated to the refractive index of air-glass (RIAG), 
glass-eva (RIGE), and eva-photovoltaic cell (RIEP) interface. The reflected 
light from the interface, pq between the adjacent layers p and q is given

The amount of reflectivity from solar PV is highly influenced by the 
incidence angle. Incidence angle is defined as the angle between the 
incident solar radiation and normal to the surface of the PV plane. This 
angle can vary from 0 degrees to 90 degrees. At small values of incidence 
angle, the reflectance is low (<5%). Reflectance increases fastly after 60 
degrees, which occurs mainly during sunrise and sunset. This means that 
the usual notion of reflectance lower than 5% of sunlight is true when 
the PV module faces the Sun (Anurag et al., 2017). For the fixed-tilt solar 
PV system, high reflectance happens for a short time in a day throughout 
the year. As the incidence angle rises, the reflectivity of the PV array 
increases. Thus, it is expected that incident angles of more than 60 de­
grees result in a high reflectivity level. At large angles of incidence, PV 
modules show high reflectance compared to other surfaces. 
The reflection from the solar PV module can be specular or diffused, 
or both. Specular reflections occur when the reflected light bounces off 
from the striking surface in one direction. However, when sunlight falls 
on a specific surface, it scatters in different directions to cause diffused 
reflections. As the surface becomes polished, it shines more. Generally, 
flat and smooth surfaces such as mirrors produce specular reflections. 
Since specular reflections are concentrated, it may cause flash blindness. 
Slope error is a measure of the amount of sunlight scattered from the 
surface of the PV module. Slope error changes with the texture of the PV 
module surface. Smooth surfaces like mirror cause mainly specular re­
flections and thus have slope error closer to zero. Rough surface causes 
more diffused reflections and thus have higher slope errors (Sandia 
National Laboratories, 2018). Based on observed glare from different PV 
modules, a slope error of around ten mrad is considered as a reasonable 
value in the glare assessment. Fig. 2 depicts the specular and diffused 
reflections of light. 
The reflectivity of the PV module is the amount of incident sunlight 
that is not absorbed by it. It is also the reverse of absorbed solar radiation 
fall on the PV array. The fraction of solar irradiance absorbed by the PV 
cell is called the absorption factor. It can be found from reflection and 
transmission experiments. A photovoltaic reflectometer can be used to 
measure the reflectance spectrum of a solar cell, preferably within 
6in*6in (Protogeropoulos and Zachariou, 2010). Though modern rem­
edies can greatly reduce the amount of reflected light, flat surfaces al­
ways have a potential for glare occurrence. In general, the reflection of 
the glass in PV is about 2–4%. According to the previous research results, 
most solar panels reflect significantly less light than flat water. The

2.2. Factors that affect the solar glare 
The factors that influence solar reflections can be broadly classified 
as variable and non-variable (Fig. 4). The factors related to Sun’s posi­
tion are categorized as non-variable factors, and those related to the 
Solar PV array are grouped as variable factors. The sun’s movement is 
not controllable by human efforts. The observer can be in a fixed posi­
tion (such as officer in ATC) or follows a predefined path (such as pilot in 
aircraft). 
2.2.1. Non-variable factors 
The position of the sun and solar intensity cannot be varied by 
human intervention. The sun’s position is calculated using the angle of 
elevation and solar azimuth. The elevation or altitude angle determines 
the altitude angle of the reflection. According to the law of reflection, it

Further, the azimuth angle determines the plane along which the re­

amount of sunlight reflected from the PV module mainly depends on the 
intensity of sunlight falling on the PV array and PV surface reflectivity. 
In addition, factors such as atmospheric conditions, time of year, and 
cloud cover also influences the amount/ intensity of sunlight hitting 
solar panel (Ho et al., 2015). The intensity of sunlight that falls on the 
solar PV module site is a function of both times of day and year. The 
position of the Sun is an important influencing factor in any glare hazard 
analysis. The Sun is assumed as a point source, and it moves along a 
predictable path during the day. The position of the Sun in the sky at the 
PV plant location is a function of daytime throughout the year. It is 
measured with respect to the reflecting surface and observer point 
(Kumar et al., 2018). The maximum intensity of the sunlight can be 
taken as 1000 W/m2 (at noon on a bright cloudless day) as the rule of 
thumb (Fig. 5). Clouds of dust/haze or humidity in the atmosphere cause 
sunlight to be diffused. Since this component of sunlight (known as 
diffused irradiance) does not have any directionality, it has a negligible 
influence on the occurrence of glare. Geographic location, time of the 
year, and cloud covers also influences intensity (Ho et al., 2015). 
2.2.2. Variable factors 
Silicon is naturally reflective in nature, and surface reflection of bare 
silicon is high (over 30%). Most of the commercially available solar PV 
panels are made from silicon. The area of the module frame (aluminium) 
is very thin, and therefore reflection from the aluminium is not 
considered. The amount of reflected sunlight depends on glass material, 
its type and orientation of the solar PV array. The colour of PV surface 
and its physical composition affects the solar reflection. Light colored 
surfaces reflect most of the incident light while the reflection from dark 
colour is least. For example, the monocrystalline PV module (being 
darker) reflects less sunlight than polycrystalline one (Sreenathet al., 
2020a, 2020b, 2020c). The physical composition refers to the surface 
texture of the glass material used in the PV system. The reflection from 
deeply textured glass is lower than the normal PV module (Fig. 6). 
The amount of solar reflection depends on the position of the PV 
array relative to the Sun. The position of the PV array is defined by 
orientation of the PV module and its tilt angle (Shiva Kumar and Sud­
hakar, 2015). The tilt angle of PV array is related to incidence angle 
which in turn affects reflectivity. These values depend on the system 
design. A solar PV module may reflect 60% of the incident solar irra­
diation when it is facing the Sun at low angles. This occurs mainly for 
low tilt PV systems during sunset and sunrise.

3. Solar glare assessment: Approaches and softwares 
Different approaches to assess the glare from light sources are re­
ported in academic literatures. Glare metrics such as Unified Glare 
Rating (UGR), Visual Comfort Probability (VCP), and CIE Glare Index 
(CGI) are suitable exclusively for indoor lighting conditions and night 
driving scenarios (Kent et al., 2019). Approaches and metrics that are 
formulated for indoor / night lighting may not be necessarily applicable 
in daylight conditions. Different approaches to assess solar glare in­
tensity and impact are discussed in this section. These approaches are 
classified on the basis of necessity for site visit by the glare consultant 
(Fig. 7). 
3.1. Site visit based approaches 
3.1.1. Helicopter flyover approach 
(Ho, 2011) assessed glare from a heliostat located in the National 
Solar Thermal Test Facility in USA by means of helicopter flyover. Based 
on this assessment, it is reported that an intense glare is observed from 
the mirrors of the heliostat over 1700 m away. This helicopter approach 
is suitable for analysing glare for existing solar PV installations. The 
glare impact can be analysed for a proposed solar PV system also. The 
solar PV module is placed in random locations within the selected site. 
The tilt and orientation of PV module is varied from minimum to the 
maximum value. For each configuration, the reflectivity on observers 
such as air traffic controllers and pilots is noted from a helicopter. The 
field test with solar PV modules at the proposed site is usually done in 
coordination with authorized (airport) personnel. This approach is 
inadequate because the field test is performed only at a certain time in a 
year; however, the sun position changes in the sky throughout the entire 
year. The drawback is that the occurrence of glare around the year 
cannot be assessed in this approach. A test at a single time may miss 
glare hazards during other parts of the year.

3.2. Computer-based approaches 
3.2.1. Computational fluid dynamics based approach 
The CFD software is capable of temperature predictions using solar 
radiation and reflective elements. The temperature on a surface can be 
correlated with the intensity of reflections, and hence the impact of glare 
is assessed (Danks and Good, 2016). To name a few, software tools such 
as ANSYS, COMSOL Multiphysics, Autodesk CFD, OpenFOAM, Power­
Flow etc. can be used for CFD simulation analysis (Technology Evalua­
tion Centers, 2019). This approach is mainly suitable for analysis of 
glare from simple geometries than complex ones. The factors such as 
wind speed, material properties that influence surface temperature are 
assumed to the best possible value. The relationship between material 
and angle of incidence of incoming sun rays is not always incorporated 
during the simulation. Also, the computational effort is huge, which 
discourages the use of CFD in glare analysis. 
3.2.2. Ho et al. approach 
The input parameters are given by the user through interactive 
google maps (selection of solar PV site and observer’s locations). Based 
on these input values, glare computation is carried out on the basis of 
look back algorithm (for optical beam spread) and envelope algorithm 
(for improved glare prediction) (Sandia National Laboratories, 2018). In 
this methodology, the impact of glare is categorised based on the values 
of retinal irradiance and size of the glare source. The impact of a given 
solar reflection is assessed on the basis of retinal irradiance rather than 
the source’s luminance. The impact of glare is illustrated as colours 
namely low potential to cause after-image (green colour), potential to 
cause after-image (yellow colour) or in rare cases, retinal damage (red 
colour) (Sreenathet al., 2020a, 2020b, 2020c). The Ho et al. approach is 
employed in SGHAT tool and ForgeSolar softwares. This approach does 
not account the factors such as inter-module distance, the relative height 
between PV array and sensitive receptors, obstacles between the ob­
servers and the PV array, variations in PV module’s optical properties, 
and atmospheric conditions. This approach is limited to flat surfaces like 
solar PV arrays. 
3.2.3. Wollert and Rose approach 
In this approach, the geometrical and photometrical data was esti­
mated using values such as date and time of the year, Sun’s position, 
location & orientation of solar PV system. The photometric values are 
found using Christophe Schlick model (Rose and Wollert, 2015). The 
rendering and simulation of the study area is carried out using computer 
graphics. In this approach, the user can choose PV modules with 
different material properties. Using this method, BRDF of the reflecting 
surface is calculated approximately. BRDF represents the amount of 
light that is scattered for each incidence angle (Zhu, 2018). It is defined 
as the ratio between reflected radiance and incident irradiance at a 
particular wavelength. If the value of BRDF of a surface and incidence 
flux on that surface are known, the reflected irradiance can be calculated

Wollert and Rose presented a detailed proof of concept for Rhein- 
Main airport, Germany with the 3D scene, including source, media­

results are displayed in graphical and tabular form. The plots show the

makes the results easily understandable and transparent. However, 
quantification of the glare impact is not included in this developed

shadow) in the examined scene is illustrated in HDR rendered image. 
The captured HDR glare images must be converted to a specific format 
(such as Radiance format) using tools such as hdrscope (Wienold & 
Christoffersen, 2006). This approach has limited use as it requires 
photographs with high spatial and/or temporal fidelity. This method is 
highly accurate in the estimation of light reflection from a wide range of 
material surface properties. The computation process is slow due to the 
highly detailed nature of the lighting calculation. 
3.3.2. Reflection protractors 
In this approach, the geometric relationship between the Sun, 
reflective surface, and observer is analyzed using simple tools. It was 
initiated by Little Fair in 1987 and improvised by Hassall and others. 
These protractors are used in conjunction with sun path information and 
photographs, hand sketches or computer renderings of the reflective 
surface (Danks & Good, 2016). Once the time and duration of the Sun’s 
position at which reflections occur are found out, the intensity of the 
solar reflection is calculated using the Holladay formula [26]. Then the 
value of intensity is compared with the adapted value for the human eye 
to check for level of glare impact. This approach requires a diagram for 
each viewpoint, which makes the process laborious. The analysis is 
complicated for reflective surfaces that are curved and non-vertical in 
nature. However, it is suitable for solar PV installations as the PV array is 
flat. There is a chance for missed reflection due to the selective analysis 
of specific points on the studied area.

3.4. Commercial softwares 
The glare prediction is important for the safe deployment of solar PV 
systems in airport areas. The software helps to determine days and times 
of potential glare. The evaluation of glare impacts from PV installation is 
carried out using modelling programs and algorithms. Glare assessment 
softwares involve complicated algorithms as the glare occurrence is 
assessed round the year in minute resolution, 
Solar Glare Hazard Analysis Tool (SGHAT): was developed by Sandia 
National Laboratories under Department of Energy, United States and it 
is in line with FAA’s glare assessment guidelines (as per document 78 FR 
63276) (Ho, 2013). This prediction tool estimates the impact of solar PV 
glare on the sensitive receptors using Ho et al. approach. To the author’s 
knowledge, it is the first software that is approved by an Aviation au­
thority (FAA). It was found that SGHAT is currently available for in­
ternal Sandia use only. This restriction is in wake of new cybersecurity 
restrictions at Sandia National Laboratories. However, the source code 
and algorithms of SGHAT tool are available for commercial use through 
licensing from Sandia. The description of SGHAT tool in this study is 
based on its version 3.0 which is the latest version as per the available 
user manual. It uses interactive google maps for site selection and has 
very fast annual simulations. It allows the user to choose from five 
different types of PV module top layer material, namely smooth glass 
without ARC, smooth glass with ARC & Light textured glass without 
ARC, light textured glass with ARC and deeply textured glass during the 
simulation. Since FAA approves the use of SGHAT software for glare 
assessment of solar installations in US airports, it attributes to the trust 
on the results obtained from this software. The user can input only one 
set of view angles (downward and azimuthal) of the observer. subse­

4.4. Daylight glare probability (DGP) index 
Is an image-based glare measurement metric used in computer-based 
glare simulation and analysis techniques. The poor predictive ability of 
existing daylight glare metrices led to development of this new index. 
DGP considers the probability that a person is disturbed out of glare. It 
does not consider glare magnitude as a glare measure. DGP is developed 
by Wienold and Christoffersen based on experimental studies in various 
daylight conditions (Labib & Baltazar, 2014). (Wienold et al., 2019) 
reported that DGP is the most robust and best-performing metric in their 
study based on experimental datasets of daylight-dominated workplaces 
as it is the only metric that did not fail any of the tests. In their study, the 
DGP showed a very strong correlation (squared correlation factor of 
0.94) with the user’s response regarding glare perception. DGP is a 
function of the vertical eye illuminance, glare source luminance, its solid 
angle and its position index. In this index, total illuminance on the eye is 
also considered in the estimation of luminance ratio. A DGP metric

5. Regulatory framework on solar glare 
Some countries have released guidelines or regulations related to 
solar glare assessment. A qualitative study involving reading and pro­
cessing of these documents is carried out to draw main contributions. 
5.1. North and South America 
United States of America (USA): In 2012, the Federal Aviation 
Administration (FAA) formed a research group with the objective of 
developing a standard for assessment of glare from solar energy systems 
and find the benchmark at which the glint and glare cause problem to 
the safe operation of aviation (Ho, 2011). The FAA reports that light 
reflected from smooth, polished surfaces such as PV arrays is more 
concentrated than light reflected off rough surfaces such as roads, trees 
or choppy water. Therefore, the FAA insisted on glare assessment for the 
PV installations in the vicinity of airports. In this regard, a document 
named “Technical Guidance for the Evaluation of Selected Solar Tech­
nologies on Airports” is released in 2010, followed by an update named 
“Interim Policy, FAA Review of Federally Obligated Airports Solar Sys­
tem Projects” in 2013 (Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), 2013). In 
this document, FAA mandates No potential for glare in the existing (or 
planned) Airport Traffic Control Tower and No potential for glare or low 
potential for after-image along final approach path of aircraft. Also, FAA 
states that glare that is beyond 50 degrees from the line-of-sight of pilot 
can be excluded as it does not constitute a safety hazard. Also, a glare 
assessment is required for all airports under the National Plan of Inte­
grated Airport Systems (“termed as obligated airports”) to obtain no 
hazard determination approval if the airport lies within 5 nautical miles 
of a proposed solar project (Devita & Barrett, 2014). As of now, FAA 
approved the use of the SGHAT to exhibit the conformity with the 
benchmarks set for ocular impact as per its interim policy. 
Canada: In a publication by Transport Canada (TP1247E), guide­
lines useful for glare assessment is included in the section named 
“Aviation Land Use in the Vicinity of Aerodromes” As per these guide­
lines, glare analysis must consider the movement of the aircraft at 
landing, take-offs and during manoeuvres. It suggests the solar PV 
designer to vary orientation and tilt of solar PV modules in order to 
mitigate the adverse impact from glare. (Transport Canada, 2014). 
These are mainly applicable to airports and aerodromes located within 
3 km of the solar PV project. If glare from a proposed project is found to 
be a potential nuisance, the project can be executed by the installation of 
shading devices or barriers. To the author’s knowledge, there are no 
regulations exist at Municipal, Provincial and Federal level regarding 
glare from solar PV systems in Canada. 
5.2. European countries 
United Kingdom (UK): On December 2010, the Civil Aviation Au­
thority (UK) has issued interim guidance on solar PV systems in and 
around the aerodrome. CAA recommends to submit a safety assurance 
report (with risk assessment) on the potential impact of the proposed 
solar PV system on aviation (Civil Aviaiton Authority UK, 2010). At the 
same time, CAA has not come up with a glare assessment methodology 
and safety metrics to assess the impact of glare on airport and aircraft 
crews. CAA is examining the various studies carried out by the FAA and 
other organisations on the effects of SPV on aviation (“Information 
Alert,” 2010). It is also reported that the informal guidance issued by 
CAA will be replaced by the formal policy in the course of time. In UK, 
any proposed solar PV project within 5 km of an airport or 100 m of a 
railroad must conduct a glare assessment before installation. Also, the 
articles of Air Navigation Order (ANO) 2009 namely Article 137 
(Endangering safety of an aircraft), Article 221 (Lights liable to 
endanger), Article 222 (Lights which dazzle or distract) commented on 
glare aspects that are important to solar PV developers in UK irre­
spective of location. It states that glare with a detrimental impact on 
aviation safety must be avoided and hence it is to be taken care of by 
solar developers and Local Planning Authorities (LPA) (Civil Aviaiton 
Authority UK, 2010). Department for communities and local govern­
ment suggests mitigation of glare and dimming of lights wherever 
possible in the wake of safety concern (Communities & Local Govern­
ment, 2016). However, it is not specific to solar installations, 
Germany: The light guidelines (Licht-Leitlinie) issued by the Min­
istry of Environment defines criteria for reflective solar glare, including 
solar PV arrays. This approach determines the probability of glare based 
on the relative location of the receptor (also called as immission) to the 
solar PV system. Only the receptors located within 100 m from the PV 
system are considered to be relevant for a glare impact study. If the 
receptors are situated in the northern side of the PV system (except in 
certain cases) and south of the PV system (except wall mounted), then 
glare impact is considered as less risky and hence the assessment is 
excluded. So, these guidelines are mostly applied to the PV system and 
receptors located on the east–west axis (Ministry for Environment 
Health and Consumer Protection, 2014). An intolerable limit of glare is 
reached when glare occurs for longer than 30 min a day or 30 h per year 
in total. This guidelines involves assumptions on glare assessment such 
as the Sun is point-shaped, the modules are perfectly specular, and sun 
hour is astronomically maximum. However, a threshold value above 
which glare occurs is not mentioned in this guideline. The guidelines 
also recommend to consider opinion of the expert who conducts the 
glare simulations in certain cases. 
Austria: On 2016, the Austrian electrical engineering association 
(OVE R11-3) released the guidelines on solar glare from photovoltaic 
systems. This guideline is intended to analyse glare from the solar PV 
system and defines the clear limit for glare hazard. These regulations

to this guideline, solar reflections are considered as a nuisance only if the 
angle between the Sun and the reflection is 20 degree or the incidence

when power density of the reflection is greater than 30 W/m2 or when 
the luminance of the reflection is greater than 50,000 cd/m2 (Stick­

its duration is less than 30 min per day or the solar PV installation is 
small, or the receptor is located far away from glare source. 
5.3. Other countries 
In Australia, there is no specific regulation to glare from solar PV 
installations. But local authorities in some cities have set a solar 
reflectance limit of 20 percent from glass-façades (Danks et al., 2016). In 
Sydney, this reflectance limit is applied to all façade components. The 
Civil Aviation Safety Authority reviews air safety regulations in 
Australia, and there are suggestions to update its regulations for 
addressing glare issues from solar PV installations (Thistleton, 2013). To 
the author’s knowledge, the southeast Asian countries have not yet 
released guidelines or regulations on glare from solar installations. 
Similar to Australia, certain Southeast Asian cities such as; Singapore, 
Shanghai, and Hong Kong, have established a reflectance limit of 20 
percent from all facades (Danks et al., 2016). In European Airport Safety 
Authority (EASA) regulations, it is suggested to conduct an operational 
safety study on glare occurrence in decent, landing and taxing phases of 
aircraft (Alberto, 2017). As of 2016, a planning and development 
guidance does not exist for large scale PV systems in an Irish context. It is 
recommended to carry out glint and glare assessment of all existing and 
proposed solar PV systems to analyse the impact on the landscape. 
Documents in countries such as South Africa, Sweden have discussed 
about the glare in the context of the working environment only. In South 
Africa, the building code has not mentioned about glare and its impact. 
However, in the government notice released by the Ministry of 
Manpower, it highlights strategies to mitigate glare so that workstations 
are free from potential glare hazards. In the building code and the 
Planning & Building Act (2010:900) of Sweden, it is mentioned that 
glare affecting the health and safety must not be existing. At the same 
time, it does not mention glare impact from solar PV array specifically. 
Only a handful of countries have released documents regarding glare 
occurrence and its impact assessment. To the author’s knowledge, the 
glare assessment research carried out by FAA is the most detailed study 
till now. Also, glare reports documents that are available online adopted 
FAA’s glare guidelines. Hence it can be adopted by aviation stakeholders 
in other countries which do not have its on regulations or guidelines. In 
FAA approved SGHAT tool, the glare impact is categorised on colour 
codes. German and Austria regulations assess the limiting value of glare 
on the basis of duration. They haven’t reported a threshold limit above 
which glare is risky. In guidelines of USA, UK & Germany, the radial 
distance from airport after which glare analysis can be excluded is 
mentioned. Table 4 summaries the important aspect of the regulations/ 
guidelines on glare impact existing in different countries. 
6. Conclusions 
Solar PV installation in airports can be a threat to the safe operation 
of aircraft. In this paper, the main factors that influences glare occur­
rence is categorised as variable and non-variable factors. The variable 
factors such as location of the PV site, dimensions of the PV array, 
orientation of PV modules and coordinates of the observers can be 
adjusted to reduce the glare impact. Based on the review carried out on 
different aspects of glare occurrence from solar PV arrays, the following 
conclusions are drawn.  
• There is usual notion that PV module have low reflectance (less than 
5%). This is true only when the PV module faces the Sun. The re­
flections from solar PV surface increases exponentially above 60 
degrees incidence angle, mainly at sunrise and sunset.  
• Flash blindness due to reflected sunlight from PV modules leads to 
after-image which reduce visibility of officers such as air traffic 
controllers, pilots who are involved in high-risk activities. The 
minimum irradiance value to cause flash blindness lies between 0.01 
and 0.1 W/cm2.  
• It is observed that different methodologies are in place to assess the 
occurrence of glare from solar PV installations. It is concluded that 
the main steps of a desirable glare assessment approach are 1) the 
solar reflections that can reach the observer’s eye are identified. 2) 
the intensities (& duration) of these reflections are computed 3) the 
obtained value is compared with threshold limit to find whether it 
crosses the safe value of glare.  
• Some approaches focus on calculation of the intensity of the solar 
reflection, while others evaluate glare duration, size of glare source. 
Among the studied methodologies, Ho et al. approach is the detailed 
and validated approach which is also accepted by FAA for glare 
assessment in airport areas.  
• Among the available metrics, it was concluded that the Ho et al. 
metric is the suitable parameter for solar glare assessment in airport 
solar PV installations. It is accepted by FAA for the no glare hazard 
approval to its airport stakeholders. In addition, Daylight Glare 
Probability (DGP) metric can also provide reliable value of solar 
glare assessment in airports. 
• There is only a few prediction softwares available (mostly pro­
prietary) for assessment of solar PV glare. It was noticed that each of 
this software caters to the requirements of specific jurisdictions 
(ForgeSolar – USA, Fraunhofer ISE - German).  
• Only handful of countries have solar glare guidance in place and it 
varies with the jurisdiction of the respective country. It is understood 
that most of these documents are loosely written with few excep­
tions. Expect the FAA’s guidelines in USA, it is found that no other 
country has detailed guideline on glare methodology and safety 
standards for solar PV glare.  
• It was observed that threshold glare limit varies with countries’ 
jurisdiction especially in terms of glare duration and intensity. 
Countries such as Germany, Austria and France have defined limit 
(glare duration/ glare intensity) above which glare is considered as a 
potential hazard. However, FAA does not provide threshold safe limit 
of glare intensity and duration. Unlike guidelines of USA, the regu­
lations and guidelines of other countries are not available in English 
which might have hindered its adoption across the globe.  
• The presented information will be beneficial to designers, airport 
stakeholders, and regulators for the ease of implementation of solar 
projects in airport environment.  
• The growing interest of solar installation in airports necessitate the 
development of acceptable guidelines on solar PV glare. Any new 
glare guidelines in airport must include a detailed methodology of

strength and weakness of glare software. So, the future recommen­
dation of the work is to analyse the capability of the glare prediction 
tool, other than SGHAT and Forge Solar.  
• The existing glare prediction software’s can include provision to 
assess glare from bifacial PV modules, option to optimise solar en­
ergy generation with Zero glare and provide user-friendly reports 
with recommendations.
